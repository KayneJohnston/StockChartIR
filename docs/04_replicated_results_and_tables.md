## 3. Verification of the paper's core claim

> *Does an all-equity portfolio (50% domestic / 50% international) dominate
> age-based target-date glide paths and 60/40 portfolios in expected utility
> and capital preservation over lifetime horizons?*

In this replication the answer is **confirmed**.

| challenger | incumbent | criteria | criteria_won | criteria_tied | criteria_lost_n | strict_dominance | criteria_lost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| balanced_all_equity | target_date_fund | 21 | 20 | 1 | 0 | yes | - |
| balanced_all_equity | sixty_forty | 21 | 20 | 1 | 0 | yes | - |
| balanced_all_equity | domestic_equity | 21 | 20 | 1 | 0 | yes | - |
| balanced_all_equity | bills_only | 21 | 20 | 1 | 0 | yes | - |

The economics behind the result are visible in the panel itself and do not
depend on any modelling choice made here.

1. **Bonds are not a hedge over the historical record.** Real equity and real
   bond returns are *positively* correlated in the full panel
   (`01_country_dataset_and_sources.md`, section 8.1). The diversification a
   domestic bond sleeve is assumed to buy is a post-2000 US phenomenon, not a
   long-run developed-market one.
2. **Bonds and bills carry inflation risk that equities do not.** Both
   correlate negatively with inflation; equities are close to neutral. Over a
   68-year real-terms horizon that asymmetry compounds.
3. **De-risking happens exactly when the horizon is still long.** A glide path
   cuts equity at age 63, but the investor has 30 years of
   consumption left to fund. The glide path reduces year-to-year wealth
   volatility and *raises* the probability of running out of money.
4. **International diversification does the work that bonds are supposed to
   do.** Splitting equity across markets cuts the single-country disaster risk
   that makes 100% domestic equity the worst of the equity strategies here.

### 3.1 A result worth flagging

100% international equity scores *above* the 50/50 split on this panel. That
is a mechanical consequence of the international leg being an equally weighted
portfolio of 37 other markets while the domestic leg is a single
market: the international sleeve is simply better diversified. ACO find the
same direction and still recommend roughly 50/50 on grounds this simulation
does not model -- home bias in consumption baskets, currency-hedging costs,
taxes and the practical limits of a retail investor's foreign allocation.
Treat the 50/50 portfolio as the implementable version of the finding, not as
the unconstrained optimum.

The ranking is stable across the empirical-only panel, the `per_block` country
draw and uniform country weighting. That the Tier-A-only (fully empirical,
16-country) panel reproduces the ordering is the check that matters most: the
conclusion is not an artefact of the calibrated Tier-B extension.

The tier split says something stronger. The all-equity advantage over the
glide path is **larger** on the empirical Tier-A histories than on the
calibrated Tier-B ones, and 60/40 does relatively *better* among Tier-B
domestic markets. The synthetic countries are generated from a factor model
fitted to Tier-A, so their equity returns are less fat-tailed and less
persistent than the real thing -- they dilute the result rather than create
it. If the Tier-B extension were manufacturing the finding, this table would
show the opposite pattern.

Levels are **not** expected to match the published tables, and do not. The
paper uses a monthly, 38-country proprietary panel with country-linked labour
income, mortality risk, a Social Security benefit calibrated to the US
earnings distribution, and full recursive Epstein-Zin utility. This
replication uses annual public data, deterministic mortality, idiosyncratic
labour income and ex-ante Epstein-Zin. What replicates is the **ordering and
its economic mechanism**, which is what the paper's contribution actually is.

## 7. Limitations

1. **The Tier-B extension is calibrated, not observed.** 22 of the
   38 countries have simulated equity, bond and bill returns. Section 5
   shows the ranking survives dropping them entirely.
2. **Annual, not monthly.** Blocks are drawn in years. This coarsens the
   persistence structure relative to ACO's 120-month blocks.
3. **No mortality risk.** Every investor lives to exactly 93. Real longevity
   uncertainty would raise the cost of ruin and, if anything, strengthen the
   case against strategies with higher ruin probabilities.
4. **Labour income is not linked to the drawn country.** See
   `03_lifecycle_utility_model.md`, section 3.1. The bias runs against the
   paper's conclusion, not toward it.
5. **Epstein-Zin is evaluated ex ante.** See `03`, section 7.4. The
   specification nests CRRA exactly, so cross-strategy comparisons are
   like-for-like, but the level is not full recursive utility.
6. **No taxes, fees, or currency hedging costs.** These would fall hardest on
   the international sleeve and are the main reason to prefer 50/50 over 100%
   international in practice.
7. **The savings rate is fixed and exogenous.** Consumption during working
   life is therefore identical across strategies by construction; the analysis
   is a portfolio comparison, not a full life-cycle optimisation.
