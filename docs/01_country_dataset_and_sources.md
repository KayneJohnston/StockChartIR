## 1. What this panel is, and what it is not

The paper's own panel is a hand-collected, monthly, 38-country
developed-market database spanning 1890-2019, assembled from Global Financial
Data, Dimson-Marsh-Staunton and national statistical sources. **That database
is proprietary and is not redistributable**, so it cannot be downloaded and
re-used here. This replication therefore rebuilds the closest panel that can
be assembled from openly licensed primary data, and labels every series by
provenance:

| Tier | Countries | Equity / bond / bill returns | Inflation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | 16 | empirical (JST / JKKST) | empirical |
| **B** | 22 | calibrated proxy (factor model) | empirical where a source exists, otherwise factor model |

Read the headline results on the understanding that the Tier-A countries carry
the empirical content. Section 5 of `04_replicated_results_and_tables.md`
reruns the whole analysis on the Tier-A-only panel so the reader can see
exactly how much the calibrated extension moves the answer. Short version: it
does not move the ranking.

## 2. Country universe

The target cross-section is **38 developed markets**, matching the paper's
count. Membership rule: IMF *advanced economies* that host an investable
domestic equity market -- which excludes Andorra, Macao SAR, Puerto Rico and
San Marino -- **plus Poland**, reclassified by FTSE Russell as a Developed
Market in September 2018.

* **Tier A (16):** AUS, BEL, CHE, DEU, DNK, ESP, FIN, FRA, GBR, ITA, JPN, NLD, NOR, PRT, SWE, USA
* **Tier B (22):** AUT, CAN, HRV, CYP, CZE, EST, GRC, HKG, ISL, IRL, ISR, KOR, LVA, LTU, LUX, MLT, NZL, POL, SGP, SVK, SVN, TWN

Tier A is exactly the set of countries for which the
Jorda-Knoll-Kuvshinov-Schularick-Taylor *Rate of Return on Everything* series
carry complete equity, bond and bill total returns. Canada and Ireland appear
in the macro half of the JST database but have no return series there, so they
fall into Tier B while still using their **empirical** JST CPI.

## 4. Construction

### 4.1 Real returns

Nominal local-currency total returns are deflated by the country's own CPI, so
every number in the panel is a **purchasing-power return for a resident of
that country**:

```
R_real = (1 + R_nominal) / (1 + pi) - 1
```

Gross real returns are floored at `1e-06` so that a total
wipe-out stays representable in logs. Only Germany's hyperinflation years
reach the floor.

### 4.2 The international equity leg

For country `i` in year `t`, `intl_eq` is the real return, **in `i`'s own
consumption units**, on an equally weighted portfolio of every *other*
available market:

```
usd_gross_j    = (1 + eq_tr_j) * (xrusd_[j,t-1] / xrusd_[j,t])
others_mean_i  = mean over j != i of usd_gross_j
intl_eq_i      = others_mean_i * (xrusd_[i,t] / xrusd_[i,t-1]) / (1 + pi_i) - 1
```

That is a leave-one-out average of *gross* USD returns, translated back into
local currency and then deflated. Building the international leg **inside**
the panel -- rather than bolting it on during simulation -- is what lets the
bootstrap preserve domestic/international correlation for free: a drawn
country-year carries both legs together.

### 4.3 Tier-B calibration

1. **Inflation** is empirical wherever a source exists: JST CPI for Canada and
   Ireland, Clio-Infra CPI inflation elsewhere. Clio-Infra ends in 2010, so
   2011-2020 is filled from the model in step 2.
2. **Equity, bond and bill real returns** come from a single-factor model
   estimated on the Tier-A cross-section, `x_it = alpha_i + beta_i * f_t +
   eps_it`, where `f_t` is the equally weighted cross-country mean of that
   series. Each Tier-B country draws a *donor* Tier-A country and inherits its
   `(alpha, beta)` and its full residual covariance across equity/bond/bill,
   so the synthetic market has a realistic cross-asset correlation structure
   rather than an assumed one.
3. **Coverage** starts at a documented market-inception year, reproducing the
   staggered entry of the real developed-market universe (Singapore 1965,
   Slovenia 1990, ...) instead of pretending every market has 131 years of
   history.
4. The nominal CPI level and USD rate are cumulated from the inflation series
   under a PPP-consistent rule, which is what folds Tier-B countries into other
   countries' international leg.

Tier-B countries with short histories inherit short-sample noise: Poland,
Slovenia and Malta post extreme geometric means over 25-30 year windows drawn
from a strong era for the world equity factor. That is precisely why the
headline bootstrap weights the domestic-country draw by usable history (see
`02_multicountry_block_bootstrap.md`, section 3.2) and why the Tier-A-only
replication is reported alongside.

### 4.4 Real exchange rate against the world basket

CPI base years differ across countries, so only *changes* in `cpi_i / xrusd_i`
are comparable. The panel reports an index cumulating
`dlog(cpi_i / xrusd_i) - dlog(world basket)` from 1.0 at each country's first
observation, with the basket taken as the equally weighted cross-country mean.

### 4.5 Frequency

The engine runs at **annual** frequency, which is the frequency at which the
underlying JST returns exist. `data.emit_monthly` also writes a monthly array
in which each annual log return is split into twelve increments that sum
exactly back to the annual figure. That array adds no information and is not
used for the headline results; it exists so downstream code expecting a
monthly panel has one.

## 5. Structural gaps and market disruptions

A country-year enters the panel only when **all five** core series are
present. Wars, market closures and currency reforms therefore appear as holes
rather than as interpolated values, and -- critically -- the bootstrap in step
2 never draws a block that spans a hole. This is the "continuous available
sub-periods" protocol: a simulated investor can live through the run-up to a
closure or the aftermath of one, but never through a splice that never
happened.

### 6.6 Sanity check against the published record

The United States row is the one a reader can check from memory. The panel
puts US real equity at a **6.66% geometric /
8.39% arithmetic** annual return with 18.7%
volatility, against 1.94% for bonds and
1.39% for bills. Those line up with the
Dimson-Marsh-Staunton and Siegel long-run figures to within a few basis
points, which is the expected agreement given different index construction.

The cross-section is the point of the exercise, though. Across the
16 **fully empirical** Tier-A markets, the worst long-run developed
equity market (PRT) returned -0.12% real
per year over more than a century, against 6.66%
for the best (USA) -- a spread of
6.8
percentage points per year, compounded over a lifetime. A US-only study never
sees that spread, which is exactly the hindsight bias the paper is built to
remove. (Short-history Tier-B markets post wider extremes still, but those are
small-sample artefacts rather than evidence; see section 4.3.)
